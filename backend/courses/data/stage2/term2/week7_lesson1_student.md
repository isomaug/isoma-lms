# Week 7 - Lesson 1

## Session length
**About half of this week's plan** (you will finish in Lesson 2)

## Today you will learn
- Design 4 structured playtest observation questions before the playtest session begins
- Facilitate a silent playtest observation — watch a player use the game without interfering and record specific behavioural observations (not opinions)
- Analyse playtest feedback and identify the single highest-priority improvement based on frequency of occurrence across multiple testers
- Implement the priority improvement and retest with the same observation question to verify the fix works

## Big idea
Real software is never finished on the first build — it is improved through testing with real users. Playtesting is not about showing off a finished game. It is about discovering what does NOT work yet, so you can fix it.

## What you need
- 4 tablets with Stack KIDI — game projects from Lesson 14

## Links to English and Maths
- **English:** Observation vs opinion in writing: "The tester tapped the background instead of the character" (observation — factual, specific) vs "The tester was confused" (opinion — vague, not actionable). You rewrite 3 opinion statements as observation statements. This is a core academic writing skill. Vocabulary: observation, evidence, frequency, priority, actionable, specific, tester, facilitate, silent.
- **Maths:** Frequency analysis: after 3 testers, tally the most common issues. Which issue has the highest tally? That is the priority improvement. Calculate: if 2 of 3 testers experienced the same issue, what percentage is that? (67%.) If the fix reduces it to 1/3 testers, what is the new percentage? (33%.) Improvement = 67% − 33% = 34% reduction.

## Let's begin

### HOOK — What Playtesting Is (and Is Not) (8 min)
1. Playtest Observation Sheet (A4 for you): 4 observation questions (written by the pair before testing) + a 3-column table: Tester 1 / Tester 2 / Tester 3 with row for each question
2. Feedback Frequency Tally (A5): list of potential issues with tally marks — "Couldn't find tap area", "Confused by level transition", "Win condition unclear", "Too easy", "Too hard", "Sound not matching action", "Other: ___"
3. Priority Improvement Card (A5): "Most common issue: ___. How many testers experienced it: ___. Our fix: ___. How we will test the fix: ___."
4. Post-fix retest record (A5): "Before fix — Tester experienced: ___. After fix — Same tester now experiences: ___. Is the fix successful? YES / PARTIALLY / NO."
5. Stopwatch for you — for timing how long it takes a new player to understand the game rules without explanation
6. Follow the demonstration steps BAD playtesting: stands next to a learner playing the game, explains every step, says "No, tap THERE," corrects mistakes immediately.
7. Ask: "Was that useful? What did I find out about whether the game works?" Nothing — because the player was guided.

### DESIGN — Observation Questions (8 min)
8. Demonstrate GOOD playtesting: hands game to learner, says nothing, watches and takes notes on what the player does. Notes specific actions: "Tapped background twice before finding character. Understood Level 2 immediately after transition."
9. Key rule: "In a playtest, the designer is a SCIENTIST — they observe. They do not help, explain, or react. All emotions happen AFTER the tester has finished."
10. You write 4 observation questions on their Playtest Observation Sheet. Questions must be answerable by watching — not by asking the player.
11. Good examples: "Does the tester find the tap target within 5 seconds?", "Does the tester attempt to replay after winning?", "Does the Level 2 transition surprise the tester?"
12. Poor examples: "Did the tester enjoy it?", "Was it fun?", "Did they understand?" (These require asking — not observing.)
13. You reviews questions: "Can this be answered by watching? Yes/No." Rewrite any that require verbal input.
