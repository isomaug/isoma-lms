# Week 6 - Lesson 2

## Session length
**About half of this week's plan** (finish what you started in Lesson 1)

## Finish the week
- Identify and explain three AI ethics frameworks: Fairness (equal treatment across groups), Accountability (human oversight of algorithmic decisions), and Transparency (explainability of how decisions are made) — using examples drawn from the learner's own classifier built in T3 Week 4
- Analyse two provided real-world AI case studies set in an African/Ugandan context and evaluate each against the three ethics frameworks, identifying which principles are upheld and which are violated
- Write a formal position paper of minimum 250 words arguing for a specific AI governance recommendation addressed to a named Ugandan decision-maker (e.g. the Uganda Communications Commission, a hospital director, a school head teacher)
- Structure the position paper in a 6-part academic format: (1) Executive Summary (2–3 sentences), (2) Background and Context, (3) Analysis of the Problem using at least one ethics framework, (4) Evidence (citing both the provided case studies and the learner's own T3 W4 classifier experience), (5) Recommendation (specific, actionable, addressed to the named decision-maker), (6) Conclusion

## Continue

### INDEPENDENT WORK — Complete Position Paper and Peer Review  (50 min)
1. Decision-maker selection (5 min): distribute the decision-maker reference list. Each pair selects one decision-maker for their position paper. The choice must be justified: "We chose the National Curriculum Development Centre because Case Study 2 concerns employment — and students at school now will be the job applicants who face this AI in 10 years." This choice is recorded at the top of the position paper template.
2. Quantitative connection preparation (5 min): you retrieves their T3 W4 confusion matrix data from their USB flash drive or their notes. They calculate: (1) their classifier's overall accuracy; (2) the recall gap between the highest-recall and lowest-recall class; (3) the number of incorrect decisions yourselfifier would make per 100 inputs. These three numbers become the "Evidence" section of the position paper.
3. Position paper planning (15 min): you plan their position paper together — discussing the argument, the evidence, and the recommendation — but you write your own individual paper. Use the 6-section template. Plan each section in bullet form first before writing prose. Check plans: "Is the recommendation specific enough to be acted upon? Does it name the decision-maker, the action required, and the standard to be met?"
4. Position paper writing begins (15 min): you write the first 3 sections individually (Executive Summary, Background, Analysis). Check your work carefully: "Is your Executive Summary a summary of your whole argument in 2–3 sentences? Could a busy policy-maker read only those sentences and understand what you are recommending and why?"
5. Complete the position paper (25 min): you finish sections 4–6 (Evidence, Recommendation, Conclusion). The Evidence section must cite both case studies by name AND include at least one quantitative finding from the learner's own T3 W4 classifier with the full implication calculation. The Recommendation must be a single, specific, actionable sentence followed by 2–3 sentences of justification. The Conclusion must loop back to the Executive Summary — confirming or refining the opening claim based on the argument presented.
6. Word count check: minimum 250 words. If below 250, expand the Analysis or Evidence section — not the Conclusion. You count and write the word count at the bottom of the paper.
7. Peer review (15 min): swap position papers with a partner (not the pair partner — swap across you to get a fresh reader). Apply the 5-criterion academic peer review rubric. Criterion 1: Argument clarity — can you identify the main claim in one sentence? Criterion 2: Evidence quality — is at least one piece of evidence a specific number with a source? Criterion 3: Ethics framework application — is at least one framework named and applied correctly? Criterion 4: Quantitative connection — does the paper include a specific number from first-hand programme experience? Criterion 5: Recommendation specificity — does the recommendation name the decision-maker, the action, and the standard? Score each 1–4 and write a specific comment for every criterion.
8. Check your work carefully. Ask: "Is your recommendation one that could actually be implemented? Would the person you are addressing have the power and resources to act on it? If not, revise your recommendation."

### SHOWCASE & DEBRIEF  (30 min)
9. Revise the position paper in light of peer review (10 min): you read their peer review feedback and make a minimum of 2 documented revisions. Write "REVISED" next to each changed section and note in one sentence what was changed and why.
10. Two you read their Executive Summaries aloud (2–3 sentences each). Class listens and votes: "Does this summary make you want to read the full paper? Does it make the recommendation clear?" The goal is not consensus — it is to experience what it feels like to have your written argument evaluated in 15 seconds.
11. One pair presents their case study analysis: reads the quantitative implication they calculated (e.g. "350 creditworthy farmers per year incorrectly denied a loan") and explains which ethics principle this violates and why. Class challenges: "Could the AI company argue that 650 correct decisions out of 1,000 is acceptable? What is the counter-argument?"
12. You addresses the programme arc explicitly: "In Term One Week 6, you sorted picture cards into categories. That was the first time you did what an AI does. In Term Two Week 6, you built a classifier and investigated bias. In Term Three Week 4, you measured your classifier with precision and recall. Today you used all of that experience as evidence in a formal policy argument. You are no longer just building AI — you understand it, you can evaluate it, and you can argue for how it should be governed. That is the highest-level AI literacy this programme aims to develop."
13. Remember English link: "A position paper is the most formal, structured, and consequential piece of writing you will encounter. It is the format used in government white papers, NGO advocacy documents, and academic policy submissions. The 6-part structure — Executive Summary, Background, Analysis, Evidence, Recommendation, Conclusion — is international. You can use this structure for any policy argument on any topic for the rest of your life."
14. Remember Math link: "You used your confusion matrix as evidence in a policy document. That is what data scientists do: they translate numbers into consequences. '30% error rate' is abstract. '10,950 wrong decisions per year' is concrete. The ability to translate a statistic into a human implication is one of the most powerful skills in any professional field."
15. Exit question (written): "Complete this sentence: The most important constraint I would place on any AI system used in Uganda is ___, because ___ ." Collect as the final formative evidence of the AI thread across all three terms.
16. Submit position paper (original + revised version), peer review rubric, and ethics framework card with learner-written example via USB flash drive.

## Stay safe
Safeguarding Note: The case studies in this session discuss discrimination and inequity — including discrimination affecting smallholder farmers and job applicants. Facilitate all discussions with care: validate that these are documented, real systemic problems without allowing the discussion to slide into blame directed at specific companies, ethnic groups, or individuals. If you raise personal experiences of discrimination or inequity, acknowledge and validate the experience while keeping the discussion focused on systemic and design responses. The position paper writing should remain analytical — not a personal grievance letter. Remind you that their position papers will be shared during peer review — they should not include personal information about themselves or their families as examples.
