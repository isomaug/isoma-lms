Identify and explain three AI ethics frameworks: Fairness (equal treatment across groups), Accountability (human oversight of algorithmic decisions), and Transparency (explainability of how decisions are made) — using examples drawn from the learner's own classifier built in T3 Week 4
Analyse two provided real-world AI case studies set in an African/Ugandan context and evaluate each against the three ethics frameworks, identifying which principles are upheld and which are violated
Write a formal position paper of minimum 250 words arguing for a specific AI governance recommendation addressed to a named Ugandan decision-maker (e.g. the Uganda Communications Commission, a hospital director, a school head teacher)
Structure the position paper in a 6-part academic format: (1) Executive Summary (2–3 sentences), (2) Background and Context, (3) Analysis of the Problem using at least one ethics framework, (4) Evidence (citing both the provided case studies and the learner's own T3 W4 classifier experience), (5) Recommendation (specific, actionable, addressed to the named decision-maker), (6) Conclusion
Connect the position paper argument explicitly to a quantitative finding from the learner's own programme work: e.g. "Our classifier from T3 Week 4 achieved 70% accuracy — meaning 30% of environmental classifications were wrong. If an AI system with this accuracy were used to automatically control hospital ventilation, 3 in every 10 automated decisions would be incorrect."
Peer-review a partner's position paper using a 5-criterion academic rubric: Argument clarity, Evidence quality, Ethics framework application, Quantitative connection, Recommendation specificity

Facilitator opens with a direct statement: "You have now built three AI classifiers across this programme. You have measured their accuracy. You know their limitations. Today you stop being the engineer and become the ethicist. The question is not 'can this system work?' — you already know it can. The question is 'should it be used, and under what conditions?'"
Introduce the three ethics frameworks on the board: FAIRNESS — does the system treat all groups equally? (Connect to the DIM class recall gap from T3 W4.) ACCOUNTABILITY — can a human override or audit the system's decisions? (Connect to the STOP command in the T3 W5 data logger.) TRANSPARENCY — can the system explain why it made each decision? (Connect to the nested if/else classifier — it can explain every decision because every rule is explicit.)
Distribute the ethics framework card. Learners read the definitions and examples (3 min), then independently write their own example from their T3 W4 classifier in the blank space at the bottom of the card. "Name one specific violation of the Fairness principle in your T3 W4 classifier, using your confusion matrix data as evidence."
Distribute the case study cards. Pairs read both case studies silently (4 min). Ask: "Which ethics principle is most clearly violated in Case Study 1? Which in Case Study 2? Are they the same principle or different ones?" Take responses from 3 pairs. Do not resolve — frame as preparation for the analytical argument to follow.
Case study structured analysis (20 min): pairs analyse both case studies in writing using the ethics frameworks. For each case study: (1) Name the AI system and its purpose. (2) Identify the training data source and its limitation. (3) Apply each of the 3 ethics frameworks — state specifically which principle is upheld and which is violated, with evidence from the case study text. (4) Calculate one quantitative implication: if the loan-default AI is 65% accurate and 1,000 farmers apply per year, how many creditworthy farmers are incorrectly denied a loan? (350.) Write the calculation.
Decision-maker selection (5 min): distribute the decision-maker reference list. Each pair selects one decision-maker for their position paper. The choice must be justified: "We chose the National Curriculum Development Centre because Case Study 2 concerns employment — and students at school now will be the job applicants who face this AI in 10 years." This choice is recorded at the top of the position paper template.
Quantitative connection preparation (5 min): each learner retrieves their T3 W4 confusion matrix data from their USB flash drive or their notes. They calculate: (1) their classifier's overall accuracy; (2) the recall gap between the highest-recall and lowest-recall class; (3) the number of incorrect decisions the classifier would make per 100 inputs. These three numbers become the "Evidence" section of the position paper.
Position paper planning (15 min): pairs plan their position paper together — discussing the argument, the evidence, and the recommendation — but each learner writes their own individual paper. Use the 6-section template. Plan each section in bullet form first before writing prose. Facilitator checks plans: "Is the recommendation specific enough to be acted upon? Does it name the decision-maker, the action required, and the standard to be met?"
Position paper writing begins (15 min): learners write the first 3 sections individually (Executive Summary, Background, Analysis). Facilitator circulates: "Is your Executive Summary a summary of your whole argument in 2–3 sentences? Could a busy policy-maker read only those sentences and understand what you are recommending and why?"
Complete the position paper (25 min): learners finish sections 4–6 (Evidence, Recommendation, Conclusion). The Evidence section must cite both case studies by name AND include at least one quantitative finding from the learner's own T3 W4 classifier with the full implication calculation. The Recommendation must be a single, specific, actionable sentence followed by 2–3 sentences of justification. The Conclusion must loop back to the Executive Summary — confirming or refining the opening claim based on the argument presented.
Word count check: minimum 250 words. If below 250, expand the Analysis or Evidence section — not the Conclusion. Learners count and write the word count at the bottom of the paper.
Peer review (15 min): swap position papers with a partner (not the pair partner — swap across pairs to get a fresh reader). Apply the 5-criterion academic peer review rubric. Criterion 1: Argument clarity — can you identify the main claim in one sentence? Criterion 2: Evidence quality — is at least one piece of evidence a specific number with a source? Criterion 3: Ethics framework application — is at least one framework named and applied correctly? Criterion 4: Quantitative connection — does the paper include a specific number from first-hand programme experience? Criterion 5: Recommendation specificity — does the recommendation name the decision-maker, the action, and the standard? Score each 1–4 and write a specific comment for every criterion.
Facilitator circulates. Ask: "Is your recommendation one that could actually be implemented? Would the person you are addressing have the power and resources to act on it? If not, revise your recommendation."
Revise the position paper in light of peer review (10 min): learners read their peer review feedback and make a minimum of 2 documented revisions. Write "REVISED" next to each changed section and note in one sentence what was changed and why.
Two learners read their Executive Summaries aloud (2–3 sentences each). Class listens and votes: "Does this summary make you want to read the full paper? Does it make the recommendation clear?" The goal is not consensus — it is to experience what it feels like to have your written argument evaluated in 15 seconds.
One pair presents their case study analysis: reads the quantitative implication they calculated (e.g. "350 creditworthy farmers per year incorrectly denied a loan") and explains which ethics principle this violates and why. Class challenges: "Could the AI company argue that 650 correct decisions out of 1,000 is acceptable? What is the counter-argument?"

Today you used all of that experience as evidence in a formal policy argument. English | The formal position paper is the most demanding writing task in the programme — it requires command of a 6-part academic structure, evidence from two external sources and one first-hand experience, application of a conceptual framework (the three AI ethics principles), and a specific actionable recommendation. The Executive Summary section — 2–3 sentences that capture the entire argument — is one of the hardest writing tasks at any level: it requires complete conceptual clarity and maximum precision. The Recommendation section must be specific: not "AI should be used carefully" but "The Uganda Communications Commission should require that any AI system used in public service delivery must publish a quarterly accuracy report showing precision and recall broken down by gender, location, and income group." This standard of writing — policy recommendation language — appears in government submissions, NGO reports, and academic policy papers. The peer review rubric requires written comments on every criterion — not scores alone.
Maths | Quantitative connection in position papers — a requirement, not optional: every position paper must cite at least one specific number from the learner's own programme work. The T3 W4 classifier evaluation produced accuracy, precision, and recall figures. Learners calculate the real-world implication of their accuracy figure: if accuracy = 70% and the system makes 100 decisions per day, 30 decisions per day are wrong. Over one year: 30 × 365 = 10,950 incorrect automated decisions. At this scale, even a "good" classifier causes significant harm if the decisions are consequential. Fairness metric: if the classifier correctly classifies 80% of COMFORTABLE readings but only 50% of DIM readings, the recall gap = 80% − 50% = 30 percentage points. A 30-point recall gap between classes is prima facie evidence of unfairness — directly calculable from the confusion matrix built in T3 W4.

KEY VOCABULARY
Safeguarding Note: The case studies in this session discuss discrimination and inequity — including discrimination affecting smallholder farmers and job applicants. Facilitate all discussions with care: validate that these are documented, real systemic problems without allowing the discussion to slide into blame directed at specific companies, ethnic groups, or individuals. If learners raise personal experiences of discrimination or inequity, acknowledge and validate the experience while keeping the discussion focused on systemic and design responses. The position paper writing should remain analytical — not a personal grievance letter. Remind learners that their position papers will be shared during peer review — they should not include personal information about themselves or their families as examples.
